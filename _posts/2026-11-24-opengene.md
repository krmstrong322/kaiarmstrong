*OpenGene — a free, open-source plasmid viewer and editor that runs entirely on your own machine. Code and downloads: [github.com/krmstrong322/OpenGene](https://github.com/krmstrong322/OpenGene) (MIT)*

If you work with DNA, you probably live inside plasmid software. You open a GenBank file, stare at the circular map, plan a digest, design a couple of primers, export something for a collaborator. It's some of the least glamorous and most essential software in biology.

It's also, mostly, commercial. The tool everyone knows is excellent — and priced and licensed accordingly, which shuts out students, hobbyists, small labs and anyone who just wants to open a `.dna` file without an account, a subscription or their sequences leaving the building.

So I built **OpenGene**: a free, open-source plasmid viewer and editor that does the everyday jobs properly, runs entirely on your own computer, and uploads nothing anywhere. No account. No server. Your plasmids stay on your disk.


![The OpenGene editor: circular map, sequence view and inspector](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/krmstrong322/OpenGene/main/docs/screenshot.png)

## What it does

OpenGene opens GenBank, FASTA, EMBL and SnapGene files (and Sanger traces — `.ab1` and `.scf`), and gives you the three views you expect, kept in sync: a circular map, a linear map and the sequence itself. Hover a feature in one view and it lights up in the others.

Around that core is the stuff you actually reach for day to day:

- **Restriction digests** — the commercial enzyme catalogue, unique-cutter picking, fragment sizes, overhang types, and warnings where a site overlaps a Dam/Dcm methylation motif.
- **Primer design** — real primer3 scoring (Tm, GC, 3′ stability, hairpins, dimers), primers saved *with* the sequence and re-located automatically when you edit around them, and import/export as CSV or FASTA.
- **Auto-annotation** — point it at an unannotated sequence and it matches a bundled library of promoters, terminators, tags, resistance genes, fluorescent proteins and origins, tolerating mismatches and indels.
- **Real editing** — type to insert or replace bases, with feature coordinates maintained, circular wrap-around handled, a reading-frame guard that warns before you shift a coding sequence's frame, and full undo/redo with a visible history.
- **Exports people ask for** — maps as SVG, PNG or PDF; records back out as GenBank, FASTA, EMBL or SnapGene, with multi-part and origin-spanning features surviving the round trip.

The desktop app is a single download for macOS and Windows — no Python, no Node, no terminal. It opens with sample plasmids (pUC19 is in there, obviously) so you can poke at it before trusting it with your own.

## How it's built

Underneath, OpenGene is a deterministic Python engine (FastAPI + Biopython) with a React/TypeScript front end. The interface never edits raw sequence strings; it issues structured operations — insert, delete, annotate, design primers — and the engine validates each one and keeps features, primers and traces consistent. That split wasn't just tidiness: because the REST API is the same operation surface the UI uses, other tools can be built on top of it. An AI assistant is on the [roadmap](https://github.com/krmstrong322/OpenGene/issues/14), and it will plug into that API rather than into the core.

## The part I didn't expect: getting flagged by AI safeguards

OpenGene was built with a lot of AI assistance — it's 2026, that's how a project this size gets built by one person. And that's where the story gets odd.

Partway through development I moved the work onto a newer model, and the safeguards kept getting in the way. Not for anything exotic — for ordinary plasmid-editor plumbing. Code that parses GenBank files. Primer calculations. Restriction-enzyme logic. The vocabulary of everyday molecular biology — *plasmid*, *primer*, *enzyme*, *sequence* — sits close enough to dual-use territory that the safety systems treated routine software development as suspect, and work that had been flowing suddenly needed arguing with.

So I reverted to Opus 4.8, where the same, entirely benign development work proceeded without the friction, and that's where OpenGene was finished.

I want to be careful here, because I'm not anti-safeguard. There is a real line in biology: help designing a plasmid viewer is not the same as help with a pathogen, and I don't want models handing out the second thing. But a safeguard that can't distinguish *software about biology* from *biology that endangers people* isn't calibrated — it's just keyword-shaped. And the cost lands in a specific place: on open, defensive, everyday tooling. The people building free plasmid editors, teaching tools and lab infrastructure are exactly the people who'll quietly route around the friction or give up, while anyone genuinely motivated is not meaningfully slowed by a refused request about FASTA parsing.

If we want AI labs to take the benign bio community with them, the safeguard has to be able to tell the difference between a sequence editor and a weapon. At the moment, in my experience, the newest systems are worse at that distinction than the model I reverted to — and that regression deserves to be said out loud, because the people it affects are building the good stuff.

## Try it, break it, improve it

OpenGene is MIT-licensed. Downloads for macOS and Windows are on the [releases page](https://github.com/krmstrong322/OpenGene/releases) (the installers aren't code-signed yet, so your OS will grumble once — the install doc walks you through it). The roadmap — cloning simulation, Sanger alignment, the AI assistant — is tracked openly in the repo, and issues and pull requests are welcome.

Free tools only stay free if people use them and say what's missing. If you open plasmids for a living, I'd love to know what would make you switch.
